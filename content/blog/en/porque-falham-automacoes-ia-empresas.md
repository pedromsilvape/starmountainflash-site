In August 2025, MIT published a study on how businesses use generative AI that got a lot of attention. The researchers ran 150 interviews, surveyed 350 employees and analysed 300 deployments. Their conclusion was that around 95% of pilot projects had no measurable impact on results. A few months earlier, Gartner had predicted that more than 40% of AI agent projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, because of rising costs, unclear value or poor risk control.

These numbers deserve a calm reading. The MIT study was criticised for oversimplifying, and it mostly looks at large companies. What interests me are the reasons behind the numbers, because they show up in exactly the same way in a business of 15 people.

I'm writing about this because it's the part almost nobody talks about. Whoever sells automations shows the workflow running on day one. By month three, when nobody is looking at it any more, the subject has been forgotten.

## Automating a process nobody can explain

Picture a window and door manufacturer where quote requests come in by email, by phone and by WhatsApp. Everyone handles them their own way. One person writes them in a notebook, another replies straight away, another leaves them for the end of the day.

If you automate this as it is, the mess carries on, only faster. An automation needs rules, and here everyone has their own.

**Warning sign:** you ask two people how they do the task and get two different answers.

**How to avoid it:** write the process down step by step before you think about tools. If it doesn't fit in ten lines, or some steps "depend", the first job is to simplify. The automation comes after.

## Choosing the tool before the problem

"I saw a video about n8n, I want to get this running here." I understand the excitement, but when the conversation starts with the name of a tool, the right question goes unasked: which task is eating up the most hours?

There's something else to bear in mind. Gartner estimates that only around 130 of the thousands of vendors marketing "AI agents" actually offer them. Many products are older chatbots or assistants with a new name.

The MIT study also found that more than half of AI spending goes to sales and marketing, while the biggest return shows up in the back office: the admin, repetitive tasks that get less attention.

**How to avoid it:** start with the task. How often it happens each week, how long it takes, who does it. The tool gets chosen at the end, and sometimes the answer is a well-built spreadsheet.

## The automation sits beside the work

For MIT, this is the main cause of failure: the tool never becomes part of the way the team already works.

A simple example. You set AI to summarise customer emails. It works well, but the summary appears in a separate app, and someone has to copy it into the CRM or the orders spreadsheet. After two weeks, that person stops opening the app, because doing it by hand is quicker.

**Warning sign:** to use the automation, someone has to open one more tab, one more account, one more password.

**How to avoid it:** the result has to land where the team already is, whether that's email, the management software, the shared folder or the CRM. If the automation makes people go somewhere else, it won't get used.

## Nobody is in charge

A password changes, someone edits a field on the website form, a supplier starts sending invoices in a different format. The automation stops, and nobody notices for days.

**How to avoid it:** there has to be a name inside the business, someone who knows the automation exists and what it does. And there have to be alerts for when it fails. That's why maintenance isn't an extra.

## There are no numbers from before

Without knowing how long a task used to take, three months from now nobody can say whether the automation paid off. And whatever can't be justified is the first thing cut when the budget gets tight.

**How to avoid it:** before you touch anything, spend a week logging how often the task happens and how long it takes. Without those numbers, there's no way to know whether the investment paid off.

## There's no plan for when the AI gets it wrong

AI gets things wrong. A crooked scanned invoice, an ambiguous email, a customer who sends their request across three messages in a row. This will happen, and it shouldn't catch anyone by surprise.

What separates a useful automation from one that causes trouble is the answer to a question asked at the start: what can the AI decide on its own, and what has to go through a person?

**How to avoid it:** start with tasks where a mistake costs little. Set up an exceptions queue, where cases the AI isn't sure about wait for someone to review them. Only later, once you trust it, do you give it more autonomy.

## What about doing it all yourself?

I'm arguing my own case here, I know. But MIT found that around two in three projects built with specialised vendors succeeded, against one in three when the company built everything in-house.

That doesn't mean you can't build your own automations. A simple connection between your website form and your email can be done in an afternoon. It means the problems above don't go away because you're the one building.

## Before you go ahead, answer this

- Can I write the process in a few lines, the same for the whole team?
- Do I know how many times a week this task happens and how long it takes?
- Will the result appear where the team already works?
- Who inside the business will be responsible?
- What happens when the automation fails or the AI isn't sure?
- If this task goes wrong once, what does it cost?

If you're unsure about more than two of these, it's not time to buy anything yet. It's time to look at the process.
